# StoryScope: Research and Application

## Source

Jenna Russell, Rishanth Rajendhran, Chau Minh Pham, Mohit Iyyer, and John Wieting, [StoryScope: Investigating idiosyncrasies in AI fiction](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.03136), version 6, 2026.

## Evidence and limits

The study compares 10,272 human stories from Books3 with generated stories from five models using prompts derived from the human stories. Its narrative-feature classifier reports 93.2% macro-F1 on its held-out fiction dataset. Macro-F1 is not general accuracy, and the result does not establish performance for short passages, other genres, future models, or mixed-authorship writing. The pipeline uses model-extracted narrative features. Its style-editing experiment covers 278 Gemini-generated stories and does not prove a method for evading detectors.

Across this corpus, generated stories more often explain themes, use continuous causal chains and tidy resolutions, and express emotions through bodily metaphors. Human stories show greater narrative diversity, including more temporal discontinuity, moral ambiguity, and subplots. These are group-level tendencies with overlapping distributions, not markers of individual authorship or a universal recipe for quality.

## Editorial application

For fiction, inspect whether a narrator repeats a meaning the action already conveys, sensory details become formulaic, dialogue exists only to state a theme, or all conflicts resolve too neatly. Consider alternatives suited to the story and author's intention. Retain straightforward plots and clear endings where appropriate. Do not mechanically add flashbacks, ambiguity, named references, subplots, or reader address.

For nonfiction, transfer only the general editorial practice of examining unnecessary repetition and improving specificity. Preserve evidence, clear claims, essential explanation, procedural completeness, and the user's intended register.
